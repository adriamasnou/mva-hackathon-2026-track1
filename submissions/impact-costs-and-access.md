### 1.1 The evidence gap we will not paper over

🔻 **CORRECTED 2026-08-29. This section previously read: "There is no published cost-effectiveness
analysis with ICERs for Wilms tumour surveillance — not in Beckwith-Wiedemann, DICER1, CMMRD or
retinoblastoma." That is wrong.** **McNeil *et al.*, *Med Pediatr Oncol* 2001, PMID 11568898** is a
cost-effectiveness model for Wilms tumour and hepatoblastoma surveillance in Beckwith-Wiedemann
syndrome, reporting **cost per life-year saved**. ✅ **Full text held 2026-08-31, so the figures can
now be given rather than characterised:** modelling two hypothetical cohorts of 1,000 BWS infants
with sonography **three times yearly from birth**, it reports **$9,642 per life-year saved when
screening stops at age 4** and **$14,740 when it continues to age 7** — both within the
**< $50,000 per life-year** band it takes as the acceptable range for population cancer screening,
and robust to high/low variation in scan cost, discount rate and screening effectiveness. Its
stated conclusion: sonography in BWS **"represent[s] a reasonable strategy for a cancer screening
program"** and a trial is warranted. It is cited in **§4 of Hol 2021, the paper this
project's surveillance recommendation is built on and whose full text we have held throughout.**

**The claim was produced by searching our own notes rather than the source.** The honest, narrower
statement is: **the BWS analysis is 25 years old, its authors' own successors call an update
"warranted", and no cost-effectiveness analysis exists for MVA specifically.**

The nearest real analysis is for a different syndrome: Li-Fraumeni surveillance in Brazil
(Frankenthal IA, Alves MC, Tak C, Achatz MI, *Lancet Reg Health Am* 2022, **PMID 36776423** · doi:10.1016/j.lana.2022.100265 · PMC9904006 🔻 *identifier added 2026-08-31 — this was the only PMC-only citation in this directory, and a PMC id is invisible to this project's PMID-based citation gate, attribution screen and every field sweep, so a correct citation was sitting in a blind spot*) — **ICER $2,982 per life-year for
females and $4,185 for males**, with 64%/45% probability of cost-effectiveness at ~1× GDP per
capita. **It is not our syndrome and we do not present it as ours.** It establishes that
surveillance in a cancer-predisposition syndrome *can* be cost-effective at these magnitudes; it
does not establish that MVA surveillance is.

## 2. The access route — how a repurposed medicine would actually reach one child

An idea with no route is not an impact claim. For a UK child, the honest map is:

**a. Individual Funding Request (IFR).** The route that exists today. The panel weighs benefit and
risk, **biological plausibility**, cost and competing priorities; the clinician **must identify the
clinical markers and outcomes that will be monitored**; and the panel **may fund a conditional
trial of treatment, with continuation dependent on benefit against criteria agreed in advance.**
That conditional-trial provision is the part most proposals miss — it means a well-specified
readout is not merely good science, it is the mechanism of access.

> 🔴 **AND HERE IS THE PARAGRAPH THAT CUTS AGAINST IT, ADDED 2026-08-31 BECAUSE IT WAS MISSING.**
> Everything above is faithful to ¶41–43 of NHS England's IFR policy (PR2086). **¶39 is not, and we
> had left it out** — while our own private analysis calls it *"the sentence that decides it"*:
>
> > *"The Panel will not fund treatment in response to an IFR if it considers that it would be more
> > appropriate for the treatment to be **the subject of research trials**."*
>
> **A first-in-child repurposing proposal is close to the paradigm case that sentence excludes**,
> and the same policy's **¶34** definition of "experimental" is met **on all five limbs** by an
> NR/MVA1 proposal. Our private route table grades this **"very narrow at best"**, and its honest
> reading is that *"¶43's conditional trial-of-treatment with pre-agreed outcome markers is the
> realistic ceiling — not open-ended funding."*
>
> **Why this correction matters more than the omission it fixes.** §2.1 below says we are not
> claiming any route is open — but a generic disclaimer is not the same as naming the specific
> provision that would refuse you. **Presenting only the enabling half of a policy is how an access
> map becomes a promise.** The route stays in this document because ¶40's "similar condition" limb
> and ¶43's conditional trial are real doorways; **they are narrow, and now the narrowness is on the
> page instead of in the private tree.**

**b. The MHRA rare disease therapies framework — the route that fits, and it is a DRAFT.**
Consulted on **21 May – 30 July 2026**; **not in force.** It is technology-agnostic, **explicitly
names repurposed medicines**, sets a prevalence threshold of **1 in 50,000** — which MVA1 clears by
about three orders of magnitude — and answers the problem that blocks every ultra-rare programme:

> *"If the condition is too rare or heterogeneous to compile a natural history document, MHRA may
> treat each patient's individual medical history as their natural history."*

**MVA1 sits in that Option 3 today.** It still requires a sponsor and a development programme,
neither of which exists.

**c. Precedent — and it is narrower than we first wrote.** An MHRA-approved master protocol for
individualised therapy **opened at Great Ormond Street on 13 January 2026** (**NCT07410143**,
sponsor EveryONE Medicines Inc.). That a UK regulator will approve a *per-patient* protocol at all
is the precedent that matters here. **It is not a precedent for the route this document maps.**

### 2.1 What we are not claiming

We are not claiming any of these routes is open to this family, that anyone qualifies, or that a
candidate medicine exists worth putting through them. **The odds on the underlying experiment are
stated elsewhere and they are poor** — 25–40% for a usable hit, well under 10% for anything a child
could take. This section maps the route; it does not claim there is yet anything to send down it.
