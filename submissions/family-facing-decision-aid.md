---

> ### What this is, and what it is not
> A patient decision aid is used when **there is more than one reasonable option** and the right
> answer depends on what matters to *you*. It does three things: gives you the facts, helps you
> work out which features matter most to you, and helps you say that to your clinical team.
>
> **It does not tell you what to choose. It does not replace your clinical team.** Where we have an
> opinion we have kept it out. Where the evidence is missing we say so rather than smoothing over it.
>
> Three decisions are covered. **For each one, "carry on as we are" is listed as a real option**,
> because it usually is one.

---

## 1. How to use this

1. Read one decision at a time. They are independent.
2. In each, mark the features that matter **most to you** — the boxes in §5 are for that.
3. Take §6 to your next appointment. It is a question list, not a script.
4. Nothing here needs deciding today.

## 2. DECISION ONE — which cancer-surveillance schedule to follow

### 2.1 The situation

George's form of MVA carries a raised risk of **Wilms tumour**, a childhood kidney cancer. Wilms
tumour is reported in roughly **half** of children with *BUB1B*-related MVA (Hol *et al.* 2021,
PMID 34134020). Regular ultrasound scanning aims to find a tumour while it is small.

**Ultrasound is a test, not a treatment.** It looks for a lump in the kidney. It cannot prevent a
tumour; it can only find one earlier than symptoms would.

### 2.2 The options

| | **A — current international consensus** | **B — older UK guidance** | **C — no scheduled surveillance** |
|---|---|---|---|
| how often | every **3 months** | every **3–4 months** | — |
| until | **7th birthday** | 5th birthday | — |
| total scans | **~28** | ~17–20 | 0 |
| where it comes from | SIOP-E 2021 (PMID 34134020), re-endorsed by AACR 2024 (PMID 39264246) | UK guidance from 2006 | — |

**Option C is included because it is a real choice**, not because anyone is recommending it. Some
families of children with complex conditions choose to reduce the number of hospital visits.

### 2.3 What happens if nothing is done (the natural course)

Without surveillance, a Wilms tumour would be found when it caused symptoms — usually a swollen
abdomen, sometimes pain or blood in the urine. It would on average be **larger and at a later stage**
at that point. Wilms tumour can double in size in **as little as 11 days**, which is the stated
reason the consensus interval is 3 months rather than 3–4.

### 2.4 The good and the bad, given equal space

**Reasons a family might choose more surveillance (A)**
- Tumours found earlier are generally smaller and at an earlier stage.
- The consensus continues to age 7 because **90% of sporadic Wilms tumours have been diagnosed by
  then** — stopping at 5 leaves a window open.
- It is the schedule the current international consensus recommends for this condition.

**Reasons a family might choose less (B or C)**
- **~28 appointments over seven years.** For a young child that is repeated travel, waiting rooms,
  and being asked to lie still. §4 sets out what that adds up to.
- Scans can find things that turn out to be nothing, leading to more tests and more worry.
- Some children find scans distressing, and that distress is real even though the scan is painless.
- A family already managing many appointments may reasonably weigh this differently.

### 2.5 How often does a scan raise a false alarm?

This is the question most surveillance information leaves out, and it has an answer — though not
one measured in MVA.

> ### About **4 in 10** children under this kind of surveillance have something show up on a scan.
> ### For most of them it is not a tumour.
> In the largest series we could find, **25 of 63 children (40%)** on a Wilms-surveillance
> protocol **had an abnormality seen on ultrasound** — most often **nephromegaly**, meaning a
> kidney larger than expected or one kidney bigger than the other. **2 of the 63 (3.2%) turned out
> to be a tumour or a tumour precursor.** (Zarate *et al.*, *Am J Med Genet A* 2009,
> **PMID 19610116**.)
>
> 🔻 **Corrected 2026-08-30, and the correction matters for how you read the number.** This box
> used to say 4 in 10 have *"something found that turns out not to be a tumour"*, and that the
> abnormalities *"required further investigation"*. **Neither is what the paper says.** It reports
> an **abnormality-detection rate**, not a false-alarm rate, and it says nothing about what
> follow-up each child then had — we had added that consequence ourselves. The distinction is not
> pedantic: the commonest finding, nephromegaly, can be **a feature of the underlying condition
> being monitored for**, so counting all 25 as false alarms overstates the harm. What is fair to
> take from it is that **scans often find something, and most of the time that something is not
> cancer** — which is a real burden to weigh, and a different one from being told 40% of scans are
> wrong.
>
> ✅ **FULL TEXT NOW HELD (2026-08-31), and it confirms both halves of that correction.** What the
> 25 findings actually were:
>
> | finding on ultrasound | children |
> |---|---|
> | nephromegaly / kidney size discrepancy | **11 / 63 (17.5%)** |
> | hydronephrosis or collecting-system dilation | 4 / 63 (6.3%) |
> | echogenic changes in the kidney | 3 / 63 (4.8%) |
> | haemangiomas / haemangioendotheliomas | 2 / 63 (3.2%) |
> | enlarged spleen | 2 / 63 (3.2%) |
> | calcium deposits in kidney or liver | 2 / 63 (3.2%) |
>
> **The single commonest finding is a large kidney**, which is a feature of the overgrowth
> condition itself. **And the paper reports no follow-up data at all** — it does not say what
> further imaging, referral or procedure any of these children then had. So the *"required further
> investigation"* clause we had written is not merely unsupported by the abstract; **it is absent
> from the full paper.** We are leaving that gap visible rather than filling it.

**And a false alarm is not always harmless.** In an earlier series, **three children had a false
positive on screening and were operated on for a suspected Wilms tumour. In two the lesion proved
to be an infected renal cyst — one of those two had a whole kidney and its ureter removed** (a
radical nephroureterectomy, in an 8-month-old boy, on a CT read as a Wilms tumour "even though it
was quite cystic"). **In the third the lesion was nephroblastomatosis** — a genuine tumour
precursor, so that operation found something real. *(Full text held and read 2026-08-31; the
earlier version of this paragraph described the two cyst cases and omitted the third child, which
made the harm look slightly worse than the paper reports.)* (Choyke *et al.*, *Med Pediatr Oncol* 1999,
**PMID 10064187**).

⚠️ **These numbers come from Beckwith-Wiedemann spectrum, not MVA.** BWS is the condition in which
Wilms surveillance has been studied most; the equivalent figures for MVA **do not exist**. They are
the best available guide and they may not transfer exactly. **We are showing you the nearest real
evidence rather than leaving the column blank — but it is a neighbouring condition's evidence, and
you should weigh it as such.**

**What we still cannot tell you.** How much surveillance improves outcome *in this condition*.
There has never been a trial of surveillance in MVA, and there never will be one at this rarity.

**What we can now tell you, because the paper arrived on 2026-08-31.** In **Beckwith–Wiedemann
syndrome** — a different condition, but the one with the most surveillance data — children whose
Wilms tumour was found **by screening** had it found **earlier and at a lower stage** than children
whose tumour was not found by screening: **stage 1.5 ± 1.3 versus 2.6 ± 1.6**, at **22.8 ± 18.6
months versus 34.7 ± 20.5 months**, across **155 children with BWS and Wilms tumour** compared
against **3,665 Wilms tumours in the US national SEER registry**. The same paper estimates that
**screening until age 7 catches close to 95% of the Wilms tumours that occur in BWS**. An earlier
series found **no late-stage disease in 12 screened children against 25 of 59 (42%) unscreened**.

⚠️ **Two honest limits on that, and they matter.** **First, nobody was randomised.** Families who
screen and families who do not may differ in other ways, and finding a tumour earlier partly
*guarantees* an earlier stage without necessarily changing the outcome — that is a known trap in
screening studies, and this design cannot rule it out. **Second, this is BWS, not MVA.** We are
reasoning across conditions because there is nothing closer.
*(Mussa* et al.*, J Cancer Res Clin Oncol 2019, **PMID 31583434**; Choyke* et al.*, 2001 figures
above)* and a **cost-effectiveness model exists for
BWS** (McNeil *et al.*, *Med Pediatr Oncol* 2001, **PMID 11568898**) — again, not MVA. **Full text
held 2026-08-31:** it models scans three times a year from birth and finds **$9,642 per life-year
saved if screening stops at age 4**, **$14,740 if it continues to age 7**, both inside the range
normally judged acceptable for population cancer screening (**under $50,000 per life-year**). Its
conclusion is that sonographic screening in BWS **is a reasonable strategy and that a trial is
warranted** — i.e. the economics are not the obstacle; the evidence of benefit is.

**One thing we can say plainly:** the consensus group recommends surveillance for *all* forms of
MVA specifically **because the numbers for each individual gene are too small to act on separately**
— not because the risk is the same across them.

## 3. DECISION TWO — reproductive options, if there are future pregnancies

### 3.1 The situation

MVA of this type needs a changed copy of the gene from each parent. **Whether that is the case here
can be confirmed by a simple test on the parents**, which a genetics service can arrange. If both
parents carry a change, each future pregnancy has a **1-in-4** chance of the condition.

### 3.2 The options

| | **A — PGT-M** (embryo testing with IVF) | **B — testing during pregnancy** | **C — no testing** | **D — decide later** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| when | before pregnancy | during pregnancy | — | — |
| what it involves | IVF cycle, embryos tested before transfer | CVS or amniocentesis | — | — |
| approved in the UK? | **yes, for this exact condition** | routinely available | — | — |
| main burden | IVF is physically and emotionally demanding, and not always successful | a small procedure-related risk of miscarriage | — | — |

**D is a real option.** Nothing here has a deadline.

### 3.3 ★ A practical obstacle you would otherwise hit

Embryo testing is **already approved in the UK for this exact condition** — and **searching the
regulator's public list for the gene name returns "No data to display."**

> **Search that list for "Mosaic Variegated Aneuploidy Syndrome 1", or for the OMIM number
> 257300 — not for "BUB1B".**

That is a fault in a public index, not a statement about your eligibility. **Approval of the
condition is not the same as being eligible**: the regulator's criteria require a risk of an
affected pregnancy of 10% or more, referral by an NHS Clinical Genetics Service, and genetic
counselling first. Whether any family qualifies is a clinical decision we are not in a position to
make.

## 4. What the surveillance schedule asks of a family — counted

Clinical protocols count scans. They rarely count what the scans cost the people attending them.
Neither number below is a reason to choose either way; they are simply the part usually left out.

| | Option A (to age 7) | Option B (to age 5) |
|---|---|---|
| scan appointments | **~28** | ~17–20 |
| years of the child's life spent in a scanning schedule | **7** | 5 |
| **difference** | **~8–11 extra appointments** | |

Each appointment is typically travel, a wait, the scan itself, and the wait for a result. Multiply
by two parents where both attend, and by any siblings who must be arranged around it.

**What families themselves have said.** The consensus group asked two patient organisations — the
International WAGR Syndrome Association and the UK BWS Support Group — and their answer was not
one-sided: **surveillance visits are "stressful for some parents while reassuring for others",
anxiety "varies from child to child", and both groups reported that *not* undergoing surveillance
can also be stressful.** Time and transport were raised, but described as less important than the
anxiety. (Hol *et al.* 2021, **PMID 34134020**, §4.)

The family-experience evidence above is quoted from Hol 2021 and comes from BWS and WAGR families,
not MVA families — no study of caregiver burden specific to MVA surveillance was found.]**

## 6. Questions to take to your appointment

**About surveillance**
1. Which schedule are we following — 3-monthly to age 7, or 3–4 monthly to age 5?
2. Is it a **kidney** ultrasound or a **full abdominal** one? *(The consensus specifies kidney only.)*
3. What happens if a scan shows something uncertain?
4. What would make you change the schedule?

**About the condition**
5. Has his diagnosis been confirmed by looking at cells under a microscope, as well as by DNA?
6. Is there anything in his notes about medicines to avoid?
   *(There is a class of cancer drug — **HSP90 inhibitors** — that laboratory work suggests could be
   particularly harmful in this condition. It is our inference from published data, not a guideline,
   and it is worth having recorded.)*

**About the family**
7. Can we be tested to confirm we each carry a change?
8. Can we be referred to a clinical genetics service to discuss reproductive options?
9. Is there a registry or study we could take part in?

**And one for us**
10. Is anything in this document wrong, out of date, or not right for us?
